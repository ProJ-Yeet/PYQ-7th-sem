\Q{Problem 1: Full parameter set of an air-filled rectangular guide}{\m{6}}
{\color{sub}\footnotesize \yr{\texttt{80 Ba}} \gap$\cdot$\gap width 3 cm, 6 GHz, dominant mode}

\begin{asked}{\texttt{80 Ba} \ [6+4]}
For an air-filled rectangular waveguide with a width of 3 cm and a desired frequency of operation of 6 GHz (for dominant mode), determine cut-off frequency, cut-off wavelength, group velocity, phase velocity, propagation wavelength in the waveguide and the characteristic impedance. Explain the four basic parameters used to describe the performance of a directional coupler.
\end{asked}
\lead{Given}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Air filling: $v = c = 3\times10^8$ m/s, $\eta = \eta_0 = 120\pi\,\Omega$.
  \item Width (broad wall) $a = 3$ cm $= 0.03$ m; $f = 6$ GHz.
  \item Dominant mode of a rectangular guide: \textbf{TE$_{10}$} ($m = 1$, $n = 0$), so $b$
    is not needed.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 1: cut-off frequency and wavelength of TE$_{10}$}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $f_c = \dfrac{c}{2}\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{1}{a}\right)^2 + 0} = \dfrac{c}{2a}
    = \dfrac{3\times10^8}{2 \times 0.03} = \boxed{5\ \text{GHz}}$.
  \item $\lambda_c = 2a = \boxed{6\ \text{cm}}$ \quad (check: $c/f_c = 3\times10^8/5\times10^9 = 0.06$ m).
  \item $f = 6$ GHz $> f_c = 5$ GHz $\Rightarrow$ \mk{the guide propagates}, so the
    remaining quantities exist.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 2: the factor, computed once}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $f_c/f = 5/6 = 0.83333$; \quad $(f_c/f)^2 = 0.69444$; \quad $1 - 0.69444 = 0.30556$.
  \item $F = \sqrt{0.30556} = \boxed{0.55277}$ \quad (exactly $\sqrt{11}/6$).
  \item Free-space wavelength $\lambda = c/f = 3\times10^8/6\times10^9 = 5$ cm.
\end{itemize}
\par\penalty0\Needspace{14\baselineskip}
\lead{Step 3: every other quantity from $F$}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.9cm}L{2.7cm}L{4.3cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Quantity} & \thead{Formula} & \thead{Substitution} & \thead{Value} \\
\midrule
Group velocity & $v_g = cF$ & $3\times10^8 \times 0.55277$ & $\mathbf{1.658\times10^8}$ m/s \\
Phase velocity & $v_p = c/F$ & $3\times10^8 / 0.55277$ & $\mathbf{5.427\times10^8}$ m/s \\
Guide wavelength & $\lambda_g = \lambda/F$ & $5 / 0.55277$ & $\mathbf{9.045}$ cm \\
Impedance (TE) & $Z_{TE} = \eta_0/F$ & $376.99 / 0.55277$ & $\mathbf{682.0\ \Omega}$ \\
Phase constant & $\beta = 2\pi/\lambda_g$ & $2\pi / 0.090453$ & $69.46$ rad/m \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Checks}: $v_p v_g = 5.427\times10^8 \times 1.658\times10^8 = 9.0\times10^{16} = c^2$
    \checkmark; and $\dfrac{1}{\lambda^2} = \dfrac{1}{\lambda_g^2} + \dfrac{1}{\lambda_c^2}$:
    $400 = 122.2 + 277.8$ m$^{-2}$ \checkmark.
  \item ``Characteristic impedance'' of a TE guide is its wave impedance $Z_{TE}$; it is
    above $\eta_0$, as it must be for TE.
  \item \textbf{Rider (4 marks)}: coupling, directivity, isolation, insertion loss, with
    $I = C + D$. See theory $\S$4.8.
\end{itemize}

\Q{Problem 3: Does the guide pass the signal? Cut-off checks}{\m{3}\m{2}}
{\color{sub}\footnotesize \yr{\textbf{70 Bh}, \textbf{\texttt{80 Bh}}} \gap$\cdot$\gap one method, two instances; 70 Bh worked in full}

\begin{asked}{\textbf{70 Bh} \ [7+3]}
Define expressions for various field components of a rectangular waveguide in TE mode. Show that a 1 GHz signal cannot propagate in TE$_{10}$ made in a rectangular waveguide with a wall separation of 5 cm.
\end{asked}
\begin{asked}{\textbf{\texttt{80 Bh}} \ [1+1+2]}
What is dominant mode in a waveguide? What are the dominant modes for rectangular and circular waveguides? Does a rectangular waveguide of width 2.254 cm support the propagation of a signal having frequency 6 GHz?
\end{asked}
\lead{Method}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*., leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=0pt]
  \item Name the mode (TE$_{10}$, the dominant one) and read the broad-wall width $a$.
  \item $f_c = c/2a$.
  \item Compare: $f > f_c$ propagates; $f < f_c$ is evanescent.
  \item For ``show that'', add the attenuation: $\gamma = \alpha = \sqrt{k_c^2 - k^2}$ is real.
\end{enumerate}
\lead{70 Bh worked: $a = 5$ cm, $f = 1$ GHz}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $f_c = \dfrac{3\times10^8}{2\times0.05} = 3\times10^9$ Hz $= \boxed{3\ \text{GHz}}$.
  \item $f = 1$ GHz $< f_c = 3$ GHz $\Rightarrow$ below cut-off.
  \item Confirm with $\gamma$:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $k_c = \pi/a = \pi/0.05 = 62.832$ rad/m, \quad $k_c^2 = 3947.8$.
    \item $k = 2\pi f/c = 2\pi\times10^9/3\times10^8 = 20.944$ rad/m, \quad $k^2 = 438.6$.
    \item $\gamma^2 = k_c^2 - k^2 = 3947.8 - 438.6 = 3509.2 > 0$ $\Rightarrow$ $\gamma$ is
      \textbf{real}: $\gamma = \alpha = \sqrt{3509.2} = 59.24$ Np/m, $\beta = 0$.
  \end{itemize}
  \item The field decays as $e^{-59.24z}$: to $1/e$ in $1/59.24 = 1.69$ cm, i.e.
    $20\log_{10}e \times 59.24 = 514.5$ dB/m. \mk{No wave travels: the 1 GHz signal cannot
    propagate.}
\end{itemize}
\par\penalty0\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
\lead{Both instances}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{1.6cm}L{2.6cm}L{3.1cm}L{2.4cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Paper} & \thead{Given} & \thead{$f_c = c/2a$} & \thead{Compare} & \thead{Answer} \\
\midrule
\textbf{70 Bh} & $a = 5$ cm, 1 GHz & $3.00$ GHz & $1 < 3$ & \mk{cannot propagate}; $\alpha = 59.2$ Np/m \\
\textbf{\texttt{80 Bh}} & $a = 2.254$ cm, 6 GHz & $3\times10^8/0.04508 = 6.65$ GHz & $6 < 6.65$ & \mk{not supported} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{80 Bh theory part}: dominant mode = lowest cut-off mode; rectangular
    TE$_{10}$, circular TE$_{11}$ ($\S$4.2).
  \item The handwritten local example (Waveguide.pdf p6) runs the same 2.254 cm guide at
    1 GHz and reaches the same verdict with $f_c = 6.65$ GHz.
\end{itemize}

Describe magic Tee based on S-parameters. Differentiate between dominant and degenerate modes. Consider a rectangular waveguide having dimension of a = 3b and find the dominant mode among TM$_{01}$, TM$_{10}$, TM$_{11}$, TM$_{21}$, TM$_{12}$, TM$_{02}$ and TM$_{20}$.
\end{asked}
\lead{Step 1: put the cut-off formula in terms of $a$ only}

\end{itemize}

\Q{Problem 6: Derive the S-matrix of an E-plane Tee}{\m{4}\m{8}}
{\color{sub}\footnotesize \yr{\textbf{\texttt{80 Bh}}, \texttt{81 Ba}} \gap$\cdot$\gap the model 3-port derivation; learn this and Problems 7 and 8 are the same script}

\begin{asked}{\textbf{\texttt{80 Bh}} \ [4+4]}
Briefly explain the need of S-parameters and their properties in microwave analysis. Explain three-port network parameters using an example of an E-plane Tee.
\end{asked}
\lead{Given}
\begin{itemize}
  \item An E-plane (series) tee: ports 1 and 2 are the \textbf{collinear arms}, port 3 is
    the \textbf{E-arm} side arm, assumed \textbf{perfectly matched to the junction}.
  \item The junction is a passive, lossless, isotropic waveguide structure.
\end{itemize}
\figC{c3_etee.png}{0.80\textwidth}
{\footnotesize\color{sub} E-plane tee: 3D view and schematic, ports labelled as used below.
Source: RF Pulchowk, S-parameter derivation sheets.}
\lead{Step 1: write the blank matrix}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A 3-port has $3^2 = 9$ coefficients:
    \[ [S] = \begin{bmatrix} S_{11} & S_{12} & S_{13} \\
                             S_{21} & S_{22} & S_{23} \\
                             S_{31} & S_{32} & S_{33} \end{bmatrix} \tag{i} \]
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 2: impose the three physical facts}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{4.1cm}L{2.6cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Physical fact} & \thead{Equation} & \thead{Why} \\
\midrule
An input at the E-arm leaves ports 1 and 2 \textbf{$180^\circ$ out of phase}
  & $S_{23} = -S_{13}$ \hfill (ii) & The E-field of the side arm drives the two collinear arms in opposition \\
Port 3 is perfectly matched to the junction
  & $S_{33} = 0$ \hfill (iii) & Nothing incident on the E-arm is reflected \\
The junction is passive and isotropic
  & $S_{ij} = S_{ji}$ \hfill (iv) & Reciprocity: $S_{12}=S_{21}$, $S_{23}=S_{32}$, $S_{31}=S_{13}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Substituting (ii), (iii) and (iv) into (i):
    \[ [S] = \begin{bmatrix} S_{11} & S_{12} & S_{13} \\
                             S_{12} & S_{22} & -S_{13} \\
                             S_{13} & -S_{13} & 0 \end{bmatrix} \tag{v} \]
  \item \mk{Four unknowns remain}: $S_{11}$, $S_{12}$, $S_{22}$, $S_{13}$. Four equations
    are needed, and the unitary property supplies them.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 3: apply the unitary (lossless) property}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A lossless network satisfies $[S][S]^{*T} = [I]$, and since (v) is symmetric this
    is simply $[S][S]^{*} = [I]$:
    \[ \begin{bmatrix} S_{11} & S_{12} & S_{13} \\ S_{12} & S_{22} & -S_{13} \\
       S_{13} & -S_{13} & 0 \end{bmatrix}
       \begin{bmatrix} S_{11}^{*} & S_{12}^{*} & S_{13}^{*} \\
       S_{12}^{*} & S_{22}^{*} & -S_{13}^{*} \\
       S_{13}^{*} & -S_{13}^{*} & 0 \end{bmatrix}
       = \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0 \\ 0&1&0 \\ 0&0&1 \end{bmatrix} \]
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 4: read off one equation per row-column product}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{1.6cm}L{5.6cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Product} & \thead{Equation} & \thead{What it gives} \\
\midrule
$R_3 \cdot C_3$ & $|S_{13}|^2 + |S_{13}|^2 = 1 \Rightarrow 2|S_{13}|^2 = 1$
  & $S_{13} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ \hfill (vi) \\
$R_3 \cdot C_1$ & $S_{13}S_{11}^{*} - S_{13}S_{12}^{*} + 0 = 0
  \Rightarrow S_{11}^{*} = S_{12}^{*}$ & $S_{11} = S_{12}$ \hfill (vii) \\
$R_1 \cdot C_1$ & $|S_{11}|^2 + |S_{12}|^2 + |S_{13}|^2 = 1$, and with (vi) and (vii)
  $2|S_{11}|^2 = 1 - \tfrac{1}{2}$ & $|S_{11}|^2 = \tfrac{1}{4}$, so
  $S_{11} = S_{12} = \tfrac{1}{2}$ \\
$R_2 \cdot C_2$ & $|S_{12}|^2 + |S_{22}|^2 + |S_{13}|^2 = 1$, i.e.
  $\tfrac{1}{4} + |S_{22}|^2 + \tfrac{1}{2} = 1$ & $|S_{22}|^2 = \tfrac{1}{4}$, so
  $S_{22} = \tfrac{1}{2}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\lead{Answer}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Substituting all four values back into (v):
    \[ \boxed{\;[S]_{\text{E-tee}} = \begin{bmatrix}
       \tfrac{1}{2} & \tfrac{1}{2} & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\[3pt]
       \tfrac{1}{2} & \tfrac{1}{2} & -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\[3pt]
       \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & 0 \end{bmatrix}\;} \]
\end{itemize}
\lead{Reading the answer back (this is the ``explain'' half of the marks)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Power split}: $|S_{13}|^2 = |S_{23}|^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}$, so an input at the
    E-arm divides \textbf{equally} between the collinear arms.
  \item \textbf{Phase}: $S_{23} = -S_{13}$, so those two outputs are \textbf{antiphase};
    this is what makes the E-tee a \textbf{difference} junction.
  \item \textbf{Matching}: $S_{33} = 0$ but $S_{11} = S_{22} = \tfrac{1}{2}$, so the
    collinear arms are \mk{not matched}: a quarter of the power incident on port 1 comes
    straight back. That is the 3-port theorem of $\S$3.5 appearing as arithmetic.
  \item \textbf{Isolation}: $S_{12} = \tfrac{1}{2} \neq 0$, so the collinear arms are
    \textbf{not isolated} from each other either. Adding the H-arm to fix both defects is
    exactly what turns a tee into a magic tee (Problem 8).
  \item \textbf{Check}: every column sums to
    $\tfrac{1}{4}+\tfrac{1}{4}+\tfrac{1}{2} = 1$ \checkmark lossless.
\end{itemize}

\Q{Problem 7: Derive the S-matrix of an H-plane Tee}{\m{8}}
{\color{sub}\footnotesize \yr{\texttt{81 Ba}} \gap$\cdot$\gap the same script as Problem 6 with one sign changed}

\begin{asked}{\texttt{81 Ba} \ [8]}
Prepare S-matrices of a perfectly working E-plane Tee, H-plane Tee and a Duplexer, and explore their uses.
\end{asked}
\lead{Given}
\begin{itemize}
  \item An H-plane (shunt) tee: ports 1 and 2 collinear, port 3 the \textbf{H-arm} side
    arm, perfectly matched to the junction.
\end{itemize}
\figC{c3_htee.png}{0.62\textwidth}
{\footnotesize\color{sub} H-plane tee: the side arm lies in the plane of the magnetic
field. Source: RF Pulchowk, S-parameter derivation sheets.}
\lead{Step 1: the one fact that differs from Problem 6}
\begin{itemize}
  \item In an H-plane tee an input at the side arm reaches the two collinear arms
    \textbf{in phase}:
    \[ S_{13} = S_{23} \tag{ii$'$} \]
  \item Everything else is unchanged: $S_{33} = 0$ (iii) and $S_{ij} = S_{ji}$ (iv).
  \item So the working matrix is
    \[ [S] = \begin{bmatrix} S_{11} & S_{12} & S_{13} \\
                             S_{12} & S_{22} & S_{13} \\
                             S_{13} & S_{13} & 0 \end{bmatrix} \tag{v$'$} \]
    with the same four unknowns.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 2: unitary property, row by row}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{1.6cm}L{5.6cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Product} & \thead{Equation} & \thead{What it gives} \\
\midrule
$R_3 \cdot C_3$ & $|S_{13}|^2 + |S_{13}|^2 = 1$
  & $S_{13} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ \\
$R_3 \cdot C_1$ & $S_{13}S_{11}^{*} + S_{13}S_{12}^{*} = 0
  \Rightarrow S_{11}^{*} = -S_{12}^{*}$ & $S_{11} = -S_{12}$ \\
$R_1 \cdot C_1$ & $|S_{11}|^2 + |S_{12}|^2 + \tfrac{1}{2} = 1
  \Rightarrow 2|S_{11}|^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}$ & $S_{11} = \tfrac{1}{2}$, so
  $S_{12} = -\tfrac{1}{2}$ \\
$R_2 \cdot C_2$ & $\tfrac{1}{4} + |S_{22}|^2 + \tfrac{1}{2} = 1$
  & $S_{22} = \tfrac{1}{2}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \mk{The sign moved.} In the E-tee the minus sign sat in the side-arm column; here
    it sits in $S_{12}$ instead. That single difference is the whole distinction between
    the two tees.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Answer, and the duplexer the same question asks for}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{H-plane tee}:
    \[ \boxed{\;[S]_{\text{H-tee}} = \begin{bmatrix}
       \tfrac{1}{2} & -\tfrac{1}{2} & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\[3pt]
       -\tfrac{1}{2} & \tfrac{1}{2} & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\[3pt]
       \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & 0 \end{bmatrix}\;} \]
  \item \textbf{Duplexer}, taken as an ideal 3-port ferrite circulator with port 1 the
    transmitter, port 2 the antenna and port 3 the receiver:
    \[ \boxed{\;[S]_{\text{duplexer}} = \begin{bmatrix}
       0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\;} \]

    \mk{deliberately non-reciprocal} ($S_{21}=1$ but $S_{12}=0$), which is the only way a

\end{itemize}

\Q{Problem 8: Derive the S-matrix of a Magic Tee}{\m{3+3}\m{8}\m{10}}
{\color{sub}\footnotesize \yr{\textbf{72 Ash}, \textbf{74 Bh}, \textbf{75 Bh}, \textbf{78 Ch}, \textbf{\texttt{81 Bh}}} \gap$\cdot$\gap \mk{the single highest-value derivation in the chapter}}

\begin{asked}{The device behind five identification questions}
Describe the operational principles of a magic tee based on its S-parameters, and derive its scattering matrix.
\end{asked}
\lead{Given}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A hybrid (magic) tee: ports 1 and 2 are the \textbf{collinear arms}, port 3 is the
    \textbf{E-arm} and port 4 is the \textbf{H-arm}. Both side arms are perfectly matched
    to the junction.
\end{itemize}
\figC{c3_magictee.png}{0.88\textwidth}
{\footnotesize\color{sub} Magic tee: an E-plane tee and an H-plane tee sharing the same
junction. Source: RF Pulchowk, S-parameter derivation sheets.}
\lead{Step 1: write the blank $4\times4$}
\[ [S] = \begin{bmatrix}
   S_{11}&S_{12}&S_{13}&S_{14} \\ S_{21}&S_{22}&S_{23}&S_{24} \\
   S_{31}&S_{32}&S_{33}&S_{34} \\ S_{41}&S_{42}&S_{43}&S_{44} \end{bmatrix} \tag{i} \]
\lead{Step 2: impose the five physical facts}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{4.5cm}L{3.0cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Physical fact} & \thead{Equation} & \thead{Why} \\
\midrule
It contains an E-plane tee, so an input at the E-arm (port 3) leaves ports 1 and 2
  \textbf{antiphase} & $S_{23} = -S_{13}$ \hfill (ii) & Same argument as Problem 6 \\
It contains an H-plane tee, so an input at the H-arm (port 4) leaves ports 1 and 2
  \textbf{in phase} & $S_{24} = +S_{14}$ \hfill (iii) & Same argument as Problem 7 \\
An input at the E-arm produces no output at the H-arm, and the reverse
  & $S_{34} = S_{43} = 0$ \hfill (iv) & The two side arms are geometrically orthogonal; the
    E-arm excites an antisymmetric field, the H-arm a symmetric one \\
Both side arms are perfectly matched to the junction & $S_{33} = S_{44} = 0$ \hfill (v)
  & Stated in the question \\
No output at port 2 for an input at port 1, and the reverse & $S_{12} = S_{21} = 0$
  \hfill (vi) & The collinear arms are decoupled, which is the ``magic'' \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{itemize}
  \item With reciprocity $S_{ij} = S_{ji}$ (vii), equations (ii) to (vii) reduce (i) to
    \[ [S] = \begin{bmatrix}
       S_{11} & 0 & S_{13} & S_{14} \\
       0 & S_{22} & -S_{13} & S_{14} \\
       S_{13} & -S_{13} & 0 & 0 \\
       S_{14} & S_{14} & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \tag{viii} \]
  \item \mk{Four unknowns}: $S_{11}$, $S_{22}$, $S_{13}$, $S_{14}$.
\end{itemize}
\par\penalty0\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\lead{Step 3: unitary property, row by row}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{1.6cm}L{5.9cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Product} & \thead{Equation} & \thead{What it gives} \\
\midrule
$R_3 \cdot C_3$ & $|S_{13}|^2 + |{-}S_{13}|^2 + 0 + 0 = 1$, i.e. $2|S_{13}|^2 = 1$
  & $S_{13} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ \\
$R_4 \cdot C_4$ & $|S_{14}|^2 + |S_{14}|^2 + 0 + 0 = 1$, i.e. $2|S_{14}|^2 = 1$
  & $S_{14} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ \\
$R_1 \cdot C_1$ & $|S_{11}|^2 + 0 + |S_{13}|^2 + |S_{14}|^2 = 1$, i.e.
  $|S_{11}|^2 + \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2} = 1$ & $|S_{11}|^2 = 0$, so
  $\mathbf{S_{11} = 0}$ \\
$R_2 \cdot C_2$ & $0 + |S_{22}|^2 + |S_{13}|^2 + |S_{14}|^2 = 1$ & $\mathbf{S_{22} = 0}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \mk{This is the striking result}: matching the two side arms and decoupling the
    collinear pair \textbf{forces} the collinear arms to be matched as well. Nothing was
    assumed about $S_{11}$ or $S_{22}$; the unitary condition produced them.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Answer}
\[ \boxed{\;[S]_{\text{magic tee}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}
   0 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 & 0
   \end{bmatrix}\;} \]
\lead{Operational principles, read straight off the matrix}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{3.0cm}L{3.8cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Drive} & \thead{Outputs} & \thead{Consequence} \\
\midrule
\textbf{E-arm} (port 3) & $b_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}a_3$, $b_2 = -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}a_3$,
  $b_4 = 0$ & Equal split, antiphase, nothing into the H-arm \\
\textbf{H-arm} (port 4) & $b_1 = b_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}a_4$, $b_3 = 0$
  & Equal split, in phase, nothing into the E-arm \\
\textbf{Port 1} & $b_3 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}a_1$, $b_4 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}a_1$,
  $b_2 = 0$ & Splits into both side arms, \textbf{nothing reaches port 2} \\
\textbf{Ports 1 and 2 together} & $b_4 \propto (a_1+a_2)$, $b_3 \propto (a_1-a_2)$
  & \mk{H-arm is the sum arm, E-arm the difference arm} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Why ``magic''}: all four ports are matched \emph{and} two pairs
    ($1$--$2$ and $3$--$4$) are completely isolated, which a 3-port can never achieve.
  \item \textbf{Uses}: power combining and splitting ($\S$4.7), balanced mixers,
    duplexers, impedance bridges, monopulse radar comparators.
  \item \textbf{In practice} the ideal matrix needs matching posts or irises at the
    junction; without them $S_{11}$ and $S_{22}$ are not zero.
\end{itemize}

\Q{Problem 9: Two magic tees joined at their E-arms}{\m{10}}
{\color{sub}\footnotesize \yr{\textbf{77 Ch}} \gap$\cdot$\gap derived by connecting two 4-port matrices; asserted in \code{wg.py}}

\begin{asked}{\textbf{77 Ch} \ [10]}
Explain the properties of two Magic Tees if one connects their E-arms and derive its S-matrix.
\end{asked}
\lead{Given: the two tees}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Each tee, ports (1, 2) collinear, 3 = E-arm, 4 = H-arm, $r = 1/\sqrt{2}$ ($\S$4.5):
    $[S_T] = r\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0&0&1&1\\ 0&0&-1&1\\ 1&-1&0&0\\ 1&1&0&0 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$.
  \item Tee A ports $1, 2, 3, 4$; tee B ports $1', 2', 3', 4'$. The E-arms $3$ and $3'$ are
    joined, so they disappear as external ports.
  \item Joining means: the wave \textbf{leaving} arm 3 \textbf{enters} arm 3', and vice
    versa: $a_{3'} = b_3$, $a_3 = b_{3'}$.
  \item External ports left: $1, 2, 4$ (tee A) and $1', 2', 4'$ (tee B) $\Rightarrow$ a
    \textbf{6-port}, $6\times6$ matrix.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 1: drive port 1 with a unit wave ($a_1 = 1$, all other inputs zero)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Inside tee A: $b_3 = S_{31} = r$, \quad $b_4 = S_{41} = r$, \quad $b_2 = S_{21} = 0$.
  \item $b_3 = r$ enters tee B's E-arm: $a_{3'} = r$.
  \item Inside tee B: $b_{1'} = S_{13}\,a_{3'} = r\cdot r = \tfrac{1}{2}$, \quad
    $b_{2'} = S_{23}\,a_{3'} = -r\cdot r = -\tfrac{1}{2}$, \quad
    $b_{4'} = S_{43}\,a_{3'} = 0$.
  \item Nothing reflects back into arm 3, because $S_{3'3'} = 0$ (matched E-arm).
  \item Column for port 1: $(b_1, b_2, b_4, b_{1'}, b_{2'}, b_{4'}) = (0,\ 0,\ r,\ \tfrac12,\ -\tfrac12,\ 0)$.
  \item Power check: $\tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = 1$ \checkmark.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 2: the other drives, same way}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Port 2: $b_3 = S_{32} = -r$, $b_4 = r$; tee B receives $-r$ $\Rightarrow$
    $b_{1'} = -\tfrac12$, $b_{2'} = +\tfrac12$.
  \item Port 4 (H-arm A): $b_1 = b_2 = r$, and $b_3 = S_{34} = 0$ $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{nothing
    crosses} to tee B.
  \item Ports $1', 2', 4'$: mirror images, by symmetry of the connection.
\end{itemize}
\par\penalty0\Needspace{16\baselineskip}
\sbsr{0.50}{%
\lead{Step 3: the 6-port S-matrix}
\[ [S] = \begin{array}{c} \\ 1 \\ 2 \\ 4 \\ 1' \\ 2' \\ 4' \end{array}
\!\!\begin{array}{c} \begin{array}{cccccc} 1 & 2 & 4 & 1' & 2' & 4' \end{array} \\
\left[\begin{array}{cccccc}
0 & 0 & r & \tfrac12 & -\tfrac12 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & r & -\tfrac12 & \tfrac12 & 0 \\
r & r & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
\tfrac12 & -\tfrac12 & 0 & 0 & 0 & r \\
-\tfrac12 & \tfrac12 & 0 & 0 & 0 & r \\
0 & 0 & 0 & r & r & 0
\end{array}\right] \end{array} \]
{\color{sub}\footnotesize $r = 1/\sqrt{2}$. Identical to the \code{connect()} result in \code{wg.py}.}}{%
\lead{Step 4: properties}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Matched}: all six diagonal entries are 0.
  \item \textbf{Reciprocal}: $[S] = [S]^T$.
  \item \textbf{Lossless}: every column's powers sum to 1.
  \item \textbf{Collinear isolation kept}: $S_{12} = S_{1'2'} = 0$.
  \item \textbf{H-arms isolated} from each other and from the far tee: $S_{44'} = 0$.
  \item \textbf{Difference channel crosses}: an antiphase pair at $(1, 2)$ appears entirely
    at $(1', 2')$, again antiphase.
  \item \textbf{Sum channel stays}: an in-phase pair at $(1, 2)$ leaves entirely by $4$.
  \item Use: a sum/difference separator, e.g. two monopulse channels feeding a common
    difference path.
\end{itemize}}

  \item Therefore row $k$ and column $k$ simply \textbf{drop out}; the rest of the matrix is

  \item Start from the magic tee, ports 1, 2 collinear (the co-planar arms), 3 = E, 4 = H.

\hrow \thead{Case} & \thead{Reduced S-matrix} & \thead{Properties} \\
\midrule
(a) E-arm terminated, ports $(1, 2, 4)$
 & $\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0&0&r\\ 0&0&r\\ r&r&0 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$
 & Matched and reciprocal. From the H-arm: \textbf{equal, in-phase} halves at 1 and 2, no
   loss. Ports 1 and 2 \textbf{isolated}. From port 1: only half reaches port 4, the other
   half is absorbed in the E-arm load, so the 3-port is \textbf{not lossless}. An in-phase

\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

\Q{Problem 12: Magic tee with both E- and H-arms short-circuited}{\m{4+4}}
{\color{sub}\footnotesize \yr{\textbf{\texttt{82 Bh}}} \gap$\cdot$\gap \added{} no source note carries this; derived here and asserted in \code{sparam.py}}

\begin{asked}{\textbf{\texttt{82 Bh}} \ [4+4]}
Design a model of a three-port network and define its characteristic parameters. Prepare the S-matrix of a rectangular magic tee having both E- and H-arms shorted, and explain how it behaves.
\end{asked}
\lead{Given}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The ideal magic tee of Problem 8, with ports 3 (E-arm) and 4 (H-arm)
    \textbf{short-circuited at the junction reference plane}.
  \item A short reflects with $\Gamma = -1$, so $a_3 = -b_3$ and $a_4 = -b_4$.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 1: write out the four scattering equations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item From $[b] = [S][a]$ with the magic-tee matrix:
    \[ b_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_3 + a_4), \qquad
       b_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(-a_3 + a_4) \]
    \[ b_3 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_1 - a_2), \qquad
       b_4 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_1 + a_2) \]
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 2: substitute the short-circuit conditions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $a_3 = -b_3 = -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_1 - a_2)$.
  \item $a_4 = -b_4 = -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_1 + a_2)$.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 3: eliminate and collect}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Into the expression for $b_1$:
    \[ b_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_1-a_2)
             - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_1+a_2)\right]
           = -\tfrac{1}{2}\left[(a_1-a_2) + (a_1+a_2)\right]
           = -\tfrac{1}{2}(2a_1) = -a_1 \]
  \item Into the expression for $b_2$:
    \[ b_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_1-a_2)
             - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_1+a_2)\right]
           = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[(a_1-a_2) - (a_1+a_2)\right]
           = \tfrac{1}{2}(-2a_2) = -a_2 \]
  \item The $a_2$ terms cancel in $b_1$ and the $a_1$ terms cancel in $b_2$: the two
    reflections arriving from the shorted arms add in the driven arm and subtract in the
    other one.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Answer}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Seen from the two collinear arms, the shorted magic tee is the two-port
    \[ \boxed{\;[S]_{\text{shorted}} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\;} \]
\end{itemize}
\lead{How it behaves}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Total reflection}: $|S_{11}| = |S_{22}| = 1$, so every watt fed into
    port 1 comes back out of port 1. Return loss $= 0$ dB, VSWR $= \infty$.
  \item \textbf{Phase reversal}: the $-1$ is a $180^\circ$ shift, exactly what an ideal
    short does. The tee behaves as a \textbf{pair of independent short circuits}, one at
    each collinear arm.
  \item \textbf{Complete isolation survives}: $S_{12} = S_{21} = 0$, so the two arms still
    cannot see each other even though both are totally reflecting.
  \item \textbf{Still lossless}: each column sums to $|-1|^2 = 1$; nothing is dissipated,
    everything is returned.
  \item \textbf{Still reciprocal}: $[S] = [S]^{T}$.
  \item \textbf{Use}: a reflective phase shifter or a calibration short. Moving the shorts
    a distance $\ell$ out from the junction changes $\Gamma$ from $-1$ to
    $-e^{-2j\beta\ell}$, and the same algebra then gives
    $S_{11} = S_{22} = -e^{-2j\beta\ell}$ with $S_{12}$ still zero, so a tunable phase
    with a constant unity magnitude.
\end{itemize}

\end{itemize}
\par\penalty0\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\lead{Step 1: run the four tests}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.15cm}L{5.3cm}L{2.2cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Test} & \thead{Computation} & \thead{Result} & \thead{Conclusion} \\

\textbf{Diagonal} & $S_{11}=S_{22}=S_{33}=S_{44}=0$ & all zero

\textbf{Transpose} & $S_{13}=S_{31}=1$, $S_{23}=S_{32}=-1$, $S_{14}=S_{41}=1$,

\bottomrule

\vspace{2pt}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Judging the condition}: divide the printed matrix by $\sqrt{2}$. Then
    every column sums to $\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2} = 1$ and the device is
    \textbf{lossless}. \mk{Say this explicitly}: it is the ``judge the condition'' mark.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 2: map the zeros and read the signs}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Zero blocks}: $S_{12} = S_{21} = 0$, so \textbf{ports 1 and 2 are
    isolated}; $S_{34} = S_{43} = 0$, so \textbf{ports 3 and 4 are isolated}. Two
    isolated pairs on a matched, lossless, reciprocal 4-port.
  \item \textbf{Column 3} reads $(1, -1)$ down the collinear ports: the signs
    \textbf{flip}, so port 3 is the \textbf{difference (E) arm}.
  \item \textbf{Column 4} reads $(1, 1)$: the signs \textbf{match}, so port 4 is the
    \textbf{sum (H) arm}.
  \item \textbf{Equal magnitudes} everywhere off the zero blocks, so every split is
    $50/50$.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Answer}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\boxed{\text{The device is a magic tee (hybrid tee)}}$, with ports 1 and 2 the
    collinear arms, port 3 the E-arm and port 4 the H-arm. Normalised:
    \[ [S] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}
       0&0&1&1 \\ 0&0&-1&1 \\ 1&-1&0&0 \\ 1&1&0&0 \end{bmatrix} \]
  \item Diagram: reproduce the 3D sketch of Problem 8 with all four arms labelled.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Properties, one line each}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Matched} at all four ports, so no port reflects: $S_{ii}=0$, return loss
    infinite in the ideal case.
  \item \textbf{Reciprocal}, $[S]=[S]^{T}$: passive waveguide, no ferrite, no gain.
  \item \textbf{Lossless} once normalised: all incident power is redistributed, none
    dissipated.
  \item \textbf{Two isolated pairs}: $1$--$2$ and $3$--$4$. Two sources on ports 1 and 2
    cannot see each other, and the E-arm and H-arm are decoupled.
  \item \textbf{Equal power division}, $|S_{13}|^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}$: every drive splits in
    half, i.e. a $3$ dB hybrid.
  \item \textbf{Phase}: E-arm drive gives antiphase outputs, H-arm drive gives in-phase
    outputs.
  \item \textbf{Uses}: power combiner and splitter, duplexer, balanced mixer, monopulse
    comparator, impedance bridge.
\end{itemize}

\begin{asked}{\textbf{75 Bh} \ [6]}
Which passive microwave device is explained by this S-matrix? Judge the condition and explain its characteristics.
$[S] = \begin{bmatrix} S_{11}&0&S_{13}&S_{14} \\ 0&S_{22}&-S_{13}&S_{14} \\ S_{13}&-S_{13}&0&0 \\ S_{14}&S_{14}&0&0 \end{bmatrix}$

\lead{Step 1: identify}
\begin{itemize}

    matched and mutually isolated.
  \item $S_{12} = S_{21} = 0$ is \textbf{already written in}, so ports 1 and 2 are

\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.15cm}L{5.0cm}L{2.4cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Test} & \thead{Computation} & \thead{Result} & \thead{Conclusion} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Diagonal} & $S_{11}$, $S_{22}$ left as unknowns; $S_{33}=S_{44}=0$
  & side arms matched & Collinear match not yet stated \\

  & $S_{14} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ & Equal, in-phase split from the H-arm \\

  & $S_{11} = 0$ & Port 1 is matched after all \\

  & $S_{22} = 0$ & Port 2 likewise \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\vspace{2pt}

  \item \textbf{Bidirectional}: being reciprocal, it combines as readily as it splits.
  \item \textbf{Limitation}: the ideal matrix assumes matching irises at the junction and
    holds over a limited bandwidth.

\Q{Problem 16: Two radar transmitters, twice the power into one antenna}{\m{6}\m{8}}
{\color{sub}\footnotesize \yr{\textbf{73 Bh}, 74 Ma} \gap$\cdot$\gap a design question answered entirely by one matrix multiplication}

\begin{asked}{\textbf{73 Bh} \ [8] \quad and \quad 74 Ma \ [6]}
Suppose there are two identical radar transmitters and a few passive devices in equipment stock. A particular application requires twice more input power to an antenna than either transmitter can deliver. As an RF engineer, give your appropriate solution with necessary figures, mathematics and sufficient explanation.
\end{asked}
\lead{Given}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Two identical transmitters, each able to deliver $P$ watts.
  \item One antenna, which must receive $2P$ watts.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 1: choose the device, and say why}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Choice}: a \textbf{magic tee} used as a power \textbf{combiner}
    (a $3$ dB hybrid coupler works identically).
  \item \textbf{Why not a simple T-junction or Y-junction}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item A plain junction gives $S_{12} \neq 0$, so each transmitter drives power into
      the other one: mutual loading, pulling of the oscillators, and possible damage.
    \item Its collinear ports are not matched ($S_{11} = S_{22} = \tfrac{1}{2}$ for a tee,
      Problem 6), so both transmitters see a high VSWR.
  \end{itemize}
  \item The magic tee cures both: $S_{12} = 0$ (perfect isolation) and $S_{ii} = 0$
    (matched at every port).
\end{itemize}
\par\penalty0\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\lead{Step 2: the connection}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.4cm}L{3.4cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Port} & \thead{Connected to} & \thead{Reason} \\
\midrule
1 (collinear) & Transmitter A & Isolated from port 2 \\
2 (collinear) & Transmitter B & Isolated from port 1 \\
3 (E-arm)     & Matched dummy load & Takes the \textbf{difference}, which is zero when the
  drives are balanced \\
4 (H-arm)     & Antenna & Takes the \textbf{sum} of the two drives \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The two feed lines must be cut to the \textbf{same electrical length}, so that the
    transmitters arrive in phase.
\end{itemize}
\par\penalty0\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\lead{Step 3: the mathematics}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Both transmitters equal and in phase, nothing incident on the side arms:
    \[ [a] = \begin{bmatrix} a & a & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}^{T},
       \qquad |a|^2 = P \]
  \item Multiply $[b] = [S][a]$ with the magic-tee matrix:
    \[ b_3 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_1 - a_2) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a - a) = 0 \]
    \[ b_4 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_1 + a_2) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(2a) = \sqrt{2}\,a \]
    \[ b_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_3 + a_4) = 0, \qquad
       b_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(-a_3 + a_4) = 0 \]
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 4: read the powers}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.4cm}L{3.6cm}L{2.0cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Port} & \thead{Wave} & \thead{Power} & \thead{Meaning} \\
\midrule
4, antenna & $b_4 = \sqrt{2}\,a$ & $|b_4|^2 = 2|a|^2 = 2P$
  & \mk{The requirement, met exactly} \\
3, dummy load & $b_3 = 0$ & $0$ & Nothing wasted when the drives are balanced \\
1 and 2, transmitters & $b_1 = b_2 = 0$ & $0$ & No reflection back into either transmitter \\
\midrule
\textbf{Total out} & & $\mathbf{2P}$ & Equals the total in, as a lossless network requires \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\par\penalty0\Needspace{7\baselineskip}
\lead{Answer}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\boxed{\text{Feed the two transmitters into the collinear arms of a magic tee,
    antenna on the H-arm, matched load on the E-arm}}$. The antenna then receives
    $2P$ watts, twice what either transmitter alone can deliver.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Explanation, which carries the remaining marks}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Why the sum appears at the H-arm}: an H-plane junction excites the two
    collinear arms in phase, so by reciprocity two in-phase drives add there.
  \item \textbf{Why the dummy load matters}: if the transmitters are unequal or out of
    phase, $b_3 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_1 - a_2) \neq 0$ and the imbalance is
    \textbf{absorbed} by the load instead of being reflected into the transmitters. The
    load must be rated for the worst-case imbalance, i.e. the full power of one
    transmitter.
  \item \textbf{Phase error costs power}: with a phase error $\phi$ between the drives,
    the antenna receives $2P\cos^2(\phi/2)$ and the load takes the rest. A $20^\circ$
    error already loses $0.13$ dB.
  \item \textbf{Alternatives}: a $3$ dB branch-line or rat-race hybrid does the same job in
    microstrip; a Wilkinson combiner does it with an isolation resistor when the device
    must be planar.
\end{itemize}

\Q{Problem 17: ASR duplexer delivering half the transmitter power}{\m{8}\m{10}}
{\color{sub}\footnotesize \yr{73 Ma, \textbf{76 Bh}} \gap$\cdot$\gap the same magic tee, driven from the other end}

\begin{asked}{\textbf{76 Bh} \ [10] \quad and \quad 73 Ma \ [8]}
A radar installation engineer is given the responsibility of installing an Airport Surveillance Radar that requires half the input power to the antenna than the transmitter can deliver, using a duplexer. Prepare its S-matrix. \quad (73 Ma: choose a suitable passive microwave device to split power in half and explain its properties.)
\end{asked}
\lead{Given}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A transmitter delivering $P$ watts.
  \item The antenna must receive $P/2$ watts, and the receiver must share the same antenna
    while staying isolated from the transmitter.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 1: choose the device}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Choice}: a \textbf{magic tee used as a duplexer}. It is the only passive
    device that splits power exactly in half \emph{and} provides the transmitter-to-receiver
    isolation a duplexer must have.
  \item A resistive splitter would also halve the power but would burn the other half and
    give no isolation.
\end{itemize}
\par\penalty0\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\lead{Step 2: the connection}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.4cm}L{3.4cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Port} & \thead{Connected to} & \thead{Reason} \\
\midrule
4 (H-arm)     & Transmitter & Drives both collinear arms equally and in phase \\
1 (collinear) & Antenna & Receives half the transmitter power \\
2 (collinear) & Matched dummy load & Absorbs the other half, and balances the bridge \\
3 (E-arm)     & Receiver & Isolated from the H-arm, so the transmit pulse never reaches it \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\lead{Step 3: transmit path}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Drive vector $[a] = \begin{bmatrix} 0&0&0&a \end{bmatrix}^{T}$ with $|a|^2 = P$.
  \item From $[b]=[S][a]$:
    \[ b_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}a_4 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}a, \qquad
       b_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}a_4 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}a, \qquad
       b_3 = 0 \]
  \item Powers:
    \[ |b_1|^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}|a|^2 = \frac{P}{2}, \qquad
       |b_2|^2 = \frac{P}{2}, \qquad |b_3|^2 = 0 \]
  \item \mk{The antenna receives exactly $P/2$}, which is the requirement, and the
    receiver on the E-arm receives \textbf{nothing} from the transmitter, which is the
    point of a duplexer.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 4: receive path}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The echo arrives at port 1 alone: $[a] = \begin{bmatrix} a_1&0&0&0
    \end{bmatrix}^{T}$ with $|a_1|^2 = P_{echo}$.
  \item $b_3 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}a_1$ and $b_4 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}a_1$, so
    \[ |b_3|^2 = \frac{P_{echo}}{2} \quad\text{to the receiver}, \qquad
       |b_4|^2 = \frac{P_{echo}}{2} \quad\text{back into the transmitter} \]
  \item \textbf{Nothing reaches port 2}, since $S_{21} = 0$.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Answer}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The duplexer is the magic tee, whose S-matrix is
    \[ \boxed{\;[S] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}
       0 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 & 0
       \end{bmatrix}\;} \]
    with port 4 the transmitter, port 1 the antenna, port 2 the matched load and port 3
    the receiver.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Properties and the honest limitation}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Equal split}: $|S_{14}|^2 = |S_{24}|^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}$, a $3$ dB hybrid.
  \item \textbf{Isolation}: transmitter (port 4) to receiver (port 3) is $S_{34} = 0$, so
    the receiver front end is protected from the full transmit pulse.
  \item \textbf{Matched at every port}, so neither the transmitter nor the antenna sees a
    standing wave.
  \item \mk{The $3$ dB penalty is real and should be stated}: half the transmit power goes
    into the dummy load, and half the echo goes back to the transmitter. The magic-tee
    duplexer therefore costs $3$ dB in each direction.
  \item \textbf{Better in practice}: a \textbf{ferrite circulator} duplexer,
    $[S] = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, routes
    the \emph{whole} transmit power to the antenna and the \emph{whole} echo to the
    receiver, with no $3$ dB loss. Quote it as the improvement whenever the question does
    not explicitly demand the half-power split, as \textbf{76 Bh} does.
\end{itemize}
