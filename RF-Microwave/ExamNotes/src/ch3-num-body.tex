\Q{Problem 1: The four tests on a numeric two-port matrix}{\m{3+1+1+1+2}}
{\color{sub}\footnotesize \yr{\textbf{\texttt{79 Bh}}} \gap$\cdot$\gap the only fully numeric S-matrix in the archive}

\begin{asked}{\textbf{\texttt{79 Bh}} \ [3+1+1+1+2]}
Why are S-parameters used in high frequencies? The S-matrix of a certain microwave network is given as
$[S] = \begin{bmatrix} 0.4+j0.5 & j0.6 \\ j0.6 & 0.4-j0.5 \end{bmatrix}$.
(a) Is the network reciprocal? (b) Is the network lossless? (c) What is the return loss at the input? (d) If the input power to the network is 5 watts, what is the reflected power?
\end{asked}
\lead{Given}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $S_{11} = 0.4+j0.5$, \quad $S_{12} = S_{21} = j0.6$, \quad $S_{22} = 0.4-j0.5$.
  \item Input power $P_{in} = 5$ W at port 1; port 2 terminated in $Z_0$.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 0: the magnitudes, computed once and reused}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $|S_{11}| = \sqrt{0.4^2 + 0.5^2} = \sqrt{0.16+0.25} = \sqrt{0.41} = 0.6403$.
  \item $|S_{22}| = \sqrt{0.4^2 + (-0.5)^2} = \sqrt{0.41} = 0.6403$ (the same).
  \item $|S_{12}| = |S_{21}| = |j0.6| = 0.6$.
  \item Squares, which is what every power test needs:
    $|S_{11}|^2 = |S_{22}|^2 = 0.41$, \quad $|S_{12}|^2 = |S_{21}|^2 = 0.36$.
\end{itemize}
\par\penalty0\Needspace{14\baselineskip}
\lead{Step 1: run the four tests}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.15cm}L{5.3cm}L{2.4cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Test} & \thead{Computation} & \thead{Result} & \thead{Conclusion} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Diagonal} & $S_{11} = 0.4+j0.5 \neq 0$, $S_{22} = 0.4-j0.5 \neq 0$
  & both non-zero & Neither port is matched \\
\textbf{Transpose} & $S_{12} = j0.6$ and $S_{21} = j0.6$
  & $S_{12} = S_{21}$ & \mk{(a) Reciprocal} \checkmark \\
\textbf{Column 1 sum} & $|S_{11}|^2 + |S_{21}|^2 = 0.41 + 0.36$
  & $0.77 \neq 1$ & \mk{(b) Not lossless} \\
\textbf{Column 2 sum} & $|S_{12}|^2 + |S_{22}|^2 = 0.36 + 0.41$
  & $0.77 \neq 1$ & Same shortfall at port 2 \\
\textbf{Column dot} & $S_{11}S_{12}^{*} + S_{21}S_{22}^{*}$
  $= (0.4+j0.5)(-j0.6) + (j0.6)(0.4+j0.5)$
  $= (0.30 - j0.24) + (-0.30 + j0.24)$ & $0$ & Phases are consistent; only the magnitudes fail \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{(a)} $S_{12} = S_{21} = j0.6$, so the network is
    $\boxed{\text{reciprocal}}$: it is passive and contains no ferrite and no active
    device.
  \item \textbf{(b)} Both columns sum to $0.77$, not $1$, so the network is
    $\boxed{\text{not lossless}}$. It \textbf{dissipates} $1 - 0.77 = 0.23$, i.e.
    \textbf{23\%} of whatever is fed into either port.
  \item Worth adding: the column-dot test \emph{passes}. A network can have the right
    phase relations and still be lossy, which is why both halves of the unitary condition
    must be checked.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 2: (c) return loss at the input}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Return loss compares incident with reflected power at port 1, and $S_{11}$ is an
    \textbf{amplitude} ratio, so the factor is $20$:
    \[ RL = -20\log_{10}|S_{11}| = -20\log_{10}(0.6403) \]
  \item $\log_{10}(0.6403) = -0.19361$, so $RL = -20 \times (-0.19361)$.
  \item \textbf{(c)} $\boxed{RL = 3.87\ \text{dB}}$.
  \item Sanity check: a low return loss means a \textbf{bad} match, and indeed
    VSWR $= \dfrac{1+0.6403}{1-0.6403} = \dfrac{1.6403}{0.3597} = 4.56$, far from 1.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 3: (d) reflected power for 5 W in}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Power scales as the \textbf{square} of the wave amplitude:
    \[ P_{ref} = |S_{11}|^2 \, P_{in} = 0.41 \times 5 \]
  \item \textbf{(d)} $\boxed{P_{ref} = 2.05\ \text{W}}$.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 4: complete the power budget (free marks)}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{3.4cm}L{4.4cm}L{2.0cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Component} & \thead{Formula} & \thead{Value} & \thead{Share of 5 W} \\
\midrule
Reflected at port 1 & $|S_{11}|^2 P_{in} = 0.41 \times 5$ & $2.05$ W & $41\%$ \\
Delivered to port 2 & $|S_{21}|^2 P_{in} = 0.36 \times 5$ & $1.80$ W & $36\%$ \\
Dissipated inside & $(1-0.77) \times 5$ & $1.15$ W & $23\%$ \\
\midrule
\textbf{Total} & & $\mathbf{5.00}$ W & $100\%$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The three rows sum to the input, which is the arithmetic check that the whole
    answer is consistent.
  \item Insertion loss, if asked: $IL = -20\log_{10}(0.6) = \boxed{4.44\ \text{dB}}$.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Answer to the theory rider (3 marks)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item S-parameters are used at high frequencies because \textbf{total $V$ and $I$ are
    not uniquely defined} when the circuit is comparable to $\lambda$, because
    \textbf{open and short terminations cannot be realised} broadband and would
    \textbf{destroy or oscillate} active devices, and because \textbf{power} is what
    microwave instruments actually measure. See $\S$3.1.
\end{itemize}

\Q{Problem 2: Comparing two amplifier S-parameter sets}{\m{4}}
{\color{sub}\footnotesize \yr{\textbf{\texttt{82 Bh}}} \gap$\cdot$\gap $Z_0 = 50\,\Omega$; a reading question, not a design question}

\begin{asked}{\textbf{\texttt{82 Bh}} \ [4]}
Find the basic differences of the systems having the following sets of S-matrix.\\
(a) $S_{11} = 0.65\angle146^\circ$, $S_{12} = 0.12\angle46^\circ$, $S_{21} = 2.30\angle44^\circ$, $S_{22} = 0.17\angle{-172^\circ}$\\
(b) $S_{11} = 0.30\angle{-134^\circ}$, $S_{12} = 0.03\angle46^\circ$, $S_{21} = 2.20\angle44^\circ$, $S_{22} = 0.10\angle{-172^\circ}$
\end{asked}
\lead{Given}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Two \textbf{two-port} sets, quoted in polar form. Only the \textbf{magnitudes}
    matter for the comparison the question asks for; the angles matter only for
    $\Delta$ and $K$.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 1: what kind of device is each? (the two tests that decide it)}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.15cm}L{4.4cm}L{3.3cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Test} & \thead{Computation} & \thead{Result} & \thead{Conclusion} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Transpose} & (a) $|S_{12}| = 0.12$ vs $|S_{21}| = 2.30$; (b) $0.03$ vs $2.20$
  & $S_{12} \neq S_{21}$ in both & \mk{Both are non-reciprocal} \\
\textbf{Column 1 sum} & (a) $0.65^2 + 2.30^2 = 0.4225 + 5.29$; (b) $0.30^2 + 2.20^2$
  & $5.71$ and $4.93$, both $\gg 1$ & \mk{Both are active}: they add power \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $|S_{21}| > 1$ means the device \textbf{has gain}, so neither set can describe a
    passive component. Both are \textbf{transistor amplifiers} at the same frequency;
    the question is which is the better one.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 2: convert every magnitude into its engineering figure}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{3.05cm}L{3.5cm}L{2.0cm}L{2.0cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Quantity} & \thead{Formula} & \thead{System (a)} & \thead{System (b)} & \thead{Better} \\
\midrule
Forward gain      & $20\log_{10}|S_{21}|$ & $7.23$ dB & $6.85$ dB & (a), barely \\
Reverse isolation & $-20\log_{10}|S_{12}|$ & $18.42$ dB & $30.46$ dB & \mk{(b), by 12 dB} \\
Input return loss & $-20\log_{10}|S_{11}|$ & $3.74$ dB & $10.46$ dB & \mk{(b)} \\
Input VSWR        & $\frac{1+|S_{11}|}{1-|S_{11}|}$ & $4.71$ & $1.86$ & \mk{(b)} \\
Output return loss & $-20\log_{10}|S_{22}|$ & $15.39$ dB & $20.00$ dB & (b) \\
Output VSWR       & $\frac{1+|S_{22}|}{1-|S_{22}|}$ & $1.41$ & $1.22$ & (b) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Sample line, so the arithmetic is visible:
    $-20\log_{10}(0.65) = -20 \times (-0.18709) = 3.74$ dB, and
    $\text{VSWR} = (1+0.65)/(1-0.65) = 1.65/0.35 = 4.71$.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 3: stability, the one place the angles are needed}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\Delta = S_{11}S_{22} - S_{12}S_{21}$, and
    $K = \dfrac{1 - |S_{11}|^2 - |S_{22}|^2 + |\Delta|^2}{2|S_{12}S_{21}|}$.
  \item \textbf{System (a)}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $S_{11}S_{22} = (0.65)(0.17)\angle(146^\circ - 172^\circ)
      = 0.1105\angle{-26^\circ} = 0.0993 - j0.0484$.
    \item $S_{12}S_{21} = (0.12)(2.30)\angle(46^\circ + 44^\circ)
      = 0.276\angle90^\circ = j0.276$.
    \item $\Delta = 0.0993 - j0.0484 - j0.276 = 0.0993 - j0.3244$, so
      $|\Delta| = 0.3393$.
    \item $K = \dfrac{1 - 0.4225 - 0.0289 + 0.1151}{2 \times 0.276}
      = \dfrac{0.6637}{0.552} = 1.202$.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{System (b)}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $S_{11}S_{22} = (0.30)(0.10)\angle(-134^\circ - 172^\circ)
      = 0.03\angle54^\circ = 0.0176 + j0.0243$.
    \item $S_{12}S_{21} = (0.03)(2.20)\angle90^\circ = j0.066$.
    \item $\Delta = 0.0176 - j0.0417$, so $|\Delta| = 0.0453$.
    \item $K = \dfrac{1 - 0.09 - 0.01 + 0.0021}{2 \times 0.066}
      = \dfrac{0.9021}{0.132} = 6.834$.
  \end{itemize}
  \item Both have $K > 1$ and $|\Delta| < 1$, so both are \textbf{unconditionally
    stable}, but (b) is stable by a wide margin and (a) only just.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Answer: the basic differences}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Both are \textbf{active, non-reciprocal two-ports}, i.e. amplifiers of nearly the
    same gain ($7.23$ dB against $6.85$ dB, a difference of $0.4$ dB that is within normal
    device spread).
  \item \textbf{The real difference is everywhere except the gain}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{(b) is far better matched at the input}: $RL$ $10.46$ dB against
      $3.74$ dB, VSWR $1.86$ against $4.71$. System (a) reflects $42\%$ of the drive power
      straight back at the source.
    \item \textbf{(b) is far better isolated}: $|S_{12}| = 0.03$ against $0.12$, an extra
      $12$ dB. System (b) is close to \textbf{unilateral}, so it can be designed with the
      simple unilateral equations; system (a) needs the full bilateral treatment.
    \item \textbf{(b) is more stable}: $K = 6.83$ against $1.20$, and $|\Delta| = 0.045$
      against $0.339$. System (a) sits near the edge of unconditional stability, so a
      mismatched load could push it into oscillation.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Verdict}: $\boxed{\text{(b) is the better amplifier}}$ at a cost of
    $0.4$ dB of gain: better match, better isolation, much better stability margin.
  \item \mk{Note for Chapter 6}: the same paper then asks for the maximum unilateral and
    bilateral gains of set (a). For the record they are $G_{TU,\max} = 9.75$ dB and
    $G_{\max} = 10.11$ dB; the derivation belongs to the amplifier chapter.
\end{itemize}

\Q{Problem 3: Derive the S-matrix of an E-plane Tee}{\m{4}\m{8}}
{\color{sub}\footnotesize \yr{\textbf{\texttt{80 Bh}}, \texttt{81 Ba}} \gap$\cdot$\gap the model 3-port derivation; learn this and Problems 4 and 5 are the same script}

\begin{asked}{\textbf{\texttt{80 Bh}} \ [4+4]}
Briefly explain the need of S-parameters and their properties in microwave analysis. Explain three-port network parameters using an example of an E-plane Tee.
\end{asked}
\lead{Given}
\begin{itemize}
  \item An E-plane (series) tee: ports 1 and 2 are the \textbf{collinear arms}, port 3 is
    the \textbf{E-arm} side arm, assumed \textbf{perfectly matched to the junction}.
  \item The junction is a passive, lossless, isotropic waveguide structure.
\end{itemize}
\figC{c3_etee.png}{0.80\textwidth}
{\footnotesize\color{sub} E-plane tee: 3D view and schematic, ports labelled as used below.
Source: RF Pulchowk, S-parameter derivation sheets.}
\lead{Step 1: write the blank matrix}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A 3-port has $3^2 = 9$ coefficients:
    \[ [S] = \begin{bmatrix} S_{11} & S_{12} & S_{13} \\
                             S_{21} & S_{22} & S_{23} \\
                             S_{31} & S_{32} & S_{33} \end{bmatrix} \tag{i} \]
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 2: impose the three physical facts}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{4.1cm}L{2.6cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Physical fact} & \thead{Equation} & \thead{Why} \\
\midrule
An input at the E-arm leaves ports 1 and 2 \textbf{$180^\circ$ out of phase}
  & $S_{23} = -S_{13}$ \hfill (ii) & The E-field of the side arm drives the two collinear arms in opposition \\
Port 3 is perfectly matched to the junction
  & $S_{33} = 0$ \hfill (iii) & Nothing incident on the E-arm is reflected \\
The junction is passive and isotropic
  & $S_{ij} = S_{ji}$ \hfill (iv) & Reciprocity: $S_{12}=S_{21}$, $S_{23}=S_{32}$, $S_{31}=S_{13}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Substituting (ii), (iii) and (iv) into (i):
    \[ [S] = \begin{bmatrix} S_{11} & S_{12} & S_{13} \\
                             S_{12} & S_{22} & -S_{13} \\
                             S_{13} & -S_{13} & 0 \end{bmatrix} \tag{v} \]
  \item \mk{Four unknowns remain}: $S_{11}$, $S_{12}$, $S_{22}$, $S_{13}$. Four equations
    are needed, and the unitary property supplies them.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 3: apply the unitary (lossless) property}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A lossless network satisfies $[S][S]^{*T} = [I]$, and since (v) is symmetric this
    is simply $[S][S]^{*} = [I]$:
    \[ \begin{bmatrix} S_{11} & S_{12} & S_{13} \\ S_{12} & S_{22} & -S_{13} \\
       S_{13} & -S_{13} & 0 \end{bmatrix}
       \begin{bmatrix} S_{11}^{*} & S_{12}^{*} & S_{13}^{*} \\
       S_{12}^{*} & S_{22}^{*} & -S_{13}^{*} \\
       S_{13}^{*} & -S_{13}^{*} & 0 \end{bmatrix}
       = \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0 \\ 0&1&0 \\ 0&0&1 \end{bmatrix} \]
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 4: read off one equation per row-column product}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{1.6cm}L{5.6cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Product} & \thead{Equation} & \thead{What it gives} \\
\midrule
$R_3 \cdot C_3$ & $|S_{13}|^2 + |S_{13}|^2 = 1 \Rightarrow 2|S_{13}|^2 = 1$
  & $S_{13} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ \hfill (vi) \\
$R_3 \cdot C_1$ & $S_{13}S_{11}^{*} - S_{13}S_{12}^{*} + 0 = 0
  \Rightarrow S_{11}^{*} = S_{12}^{*}$ & $S_{11} = S_{12}$ \hfill (vii) \\
$R_1 \cdot C_1$ & $|S_{11}|^2 + |S_{12}|^2 + |S_{13}|^2 = 1$, and with (vi) and (vii)
  $2|S_{11}|^2 = 1 - \tfrac{1}{2}$ & $|S_{11}|^2 = \tfrac{1}{4}$, so
  $S_{11} = S_{12} = \tfrac{1}{2}$ \\
$R_2 \cdot C_2$ & $|S_{12}|^2 + |S_{22}|^2 + |S_{13}|^2 = 1$, i.e.
  $\tfrac{1}{4} + |S_{22}|^2 + \tfrac{1}{2} = 1$ & $|S_{22}|^2 = \tfrac{1}{4}$, so
  $S_{22} = \tfrac{1}{2}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\lead{Answer}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Substituting all four values back into (v):
    \[ \boxed{\;[S]_{\text{E-tee}} = \begin{bmatrix}
       \tfrac{1}{2} & \tfrac{1}{2} & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\[3pt]
       \tfrac{1}{2} & \tfrac{1}{2} & -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\[3pt]
       \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & 0 \end{bmatrix}\;} \]
\end{itemize}
\lead{Reading the answer back (this is the ``explain'' half of the marks)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Power split}: $|S_{13}|^2 = |S_{23}|^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}$, so an input at the
    E-arm divides \textbf{equally} between the collinear arms.
  \item \textbf{Phase}: $S_{23} = -S_{13}$, so those two outputs are \textbf{antiphase};
    this is what makes the E-tee a \textbf{difference} junction.
  \item \textbf{Matching}: $S_{33} = 0$ but $S_{11} = S_{22} = \tfrac{1}{2}$, so the
    collinear arms are \mk{not matched}: a quarter of the power incident on port 1 comes
    straight back. That is the 3-port theorem of $\S$3.5 appearing as arithmetic.
  \item \textbf{Isolation}: $S_{12} = \tfrac{1}{2} \neq 0$, so the collinear arms are
    \textbf{not isolated} from each other either. Adding the H-arm to fix both defects is
    exactly what turns a tee into a magic tee (Problem 5).
  \item \textbf{Check}: every column sums to
    $\tfrac{1}{4}+\tfrac{1}{4}+\tfrac{1}{2} = 1$ \checkmark lossless.
\end{itemize}

\Q{Problem 4: Derive the S-matrix of an H-plane Tee}{\m{8}}
{\color{sub}\footnotesize \yr{\texttt{81 Ba}} \gap$\cdot$\gap the same script as Problem 3 with one sign changed}

\begin{asked}{\texttt{81 Ba} \ [8]}
Prepare S-matrices of a perfectly working E-plane Tee, H-plane Tee and a Duplexer, and explore their uses.
\end{asked}
\lead{Given}
\begin{itemize}
  \item An H-plane (shunt) tee: ports 1 and 2 collinear, port 3 the \textbf{H-arm} side
    arm, perfectly matched to the junction.
\end{itemize}
\figC{c3_htee.png}{0.62\textwidth}
{\footnotesize\color{sub} H-plane tee: the side arm lies in the plane of the magnetic
field. Source: RF Pulchowk, S-parameter derivation sheets.}
\lead{Step 1: the one fact that differs from Problem 3}
\begin{itemize}
  \item In an H-plane tee an input at the side arm reaches the two collinear arms
    \textbf{in phase}:
    \[ S_{13} = S_{23} \tag{ii$'$} \]
  \item Everything else is unchanged: $S_{33} = 0$ (iii) and $S_{ij} = S_{ji}$ (iv).
  \item So the working matrix is
    \[ [S] = \begin{bmatrix} S_{11} & S_{12} & S_{13} \\
                             S_{12} & S_{22} & S_{13} \\
                             S_{13} & S_{13} & 0 \end{bmatrix} \tag{v$'$} \]
    with the same four unknowns.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 2: unitary property, row by row}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{1.6cm}L{5.6cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Product} & \thead{Equation} & \thead{What it gives} \\
\midrule
$R_3 \cdot C_3$ & $|S_{13}|^2 + |S_{13}|^2 = 1$
  & $S_{13} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ \\
$R_3 \cdot C_1$ & $S_{13}S_{11}^{*} + S_{13}S_{12}^{*} = 0
  \Rightarrow S_{11}^{*} = -S_{12}^{*}$ & $S_{11} = -S_{12}$ \\
$R_1 \cdot C_1$ & $|S_{11}|^2 + |S_{12}|^2 + \tfrac{1}{2} = 1
  \Rightarrow 2|S_{11}|^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}$ & $S_{11} = \tfrac{1}{2}$, so
  $S_{12} = -\tfrac{1}{2}$ \\
$R_2 \cdot C_2$ & $\tfrac{1}{4} + |S_{22}|^2 + \tfrac{1}{2} = 1$
  & $S_{22} = \tfrac{1}{2}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \mk{The sign moved.} In the E-tee the minus sign sat in the side-arm column; here
    it sits in $S_{12}$ instead. That single difference is the whole distinction between
    the two tees.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Answer, and the duplexer the same question asks for}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{H-plane tee}:
    \[ \boxed{\;[S]_{\text{H-tee}} = \begin{bmatrix}
       \tfrac{1}{2} & -\tfrac{1}{2} & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\[3pt]
       -\tfrac{1}{2} & \tfrac{1}{2} & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\[3pt]
       \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & 0 \end{bmatrix}\;} \]
  \item \textbf{Duplexer}, taken as an ideal 3-port ferrite circulator with port 1 the
    transmitter, port 2 the antenna and port 3 the receiver:
    \[ \boxed{\;[S]_{\text{duplexer}} = \begin{bmatrix}
       0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\;} \]
    Matched at every port ($S_{ii}=0$), lossless (every column sums to 1) and
    \mk{deliberately non-reciprocal} ($S_{21}=1$ but $S_{12}=0$), which is the only way a
    3-port can be both matched and lossless ($\S$3.5).
\end{itemize}
\lead{Uses, which the question asks for explicitly}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.4cm}L{3.5cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Device} & \thead{Behaviour} & \thead{Uses} \\
\midrule
\textbf{E-plane tee} & Equal split, outputs in antiphase; difference junction
  & Balanced mixers, phase comparators, the difference arm of a magic tee, feeding
    balanced antennas \\
\textbf{H-plane tee} & Equal split, outputs in phase; sum junction
  & In-phase power dividers and combiners, feeding two antennas from one source, the
    sum arm of a magic tee \\
\textbf{Duplexer} & One antenna shared by transmitter and receiver, with the receiver
  isolated from the transmitter & Radar, and any single-antenna transceiver; protects the
  sensitive receiver front end from the transmit pulse \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

\Q{Problem 5: Derive the S-matrix of a Magic Tee}{\m{3+3}\m{8}\m{10}}
{\color{sub}\footnotesize \yr{\textbf{72 Ash}, \textbf{74 Bh}, \textbf{75 Bh}, \textbf{78 Ch}, \textbf{\texttt{81 Bh}}} \gap$\cdot$\gap \mk{the single highest-value derivation in the chapter}}

\begin{asked}{The device behind five identification questions}
Describe the operational principles of a magic tee based on its S-parameters, and derive its scattering matrix.
\end{asked}
\lead{Given}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A hybrid (magic) tee: ports 1 and 2 are the \textbf{collinear arms}, port 3 is the
    \textbf{E-arm} and port 4 is the \textbf{H-arm}. Both side arms are perfectly matched
    to the junction.
\end{itemize}
\figC{c3_magictee.png}{0.88\textwidth}
{\footnotesize\color{sub} Magic tee: an E-plane tee and an H-plane tee sharing the same

{\color{sub}\footnotesize \yr{\textbf{78 Ch}} \gap$\cdot$\gap the printed matrix is missing its normalising factor}

\begin{asked}{\textbf{78 Ch} \ [10]}
Identify and explain the properties of a microwave passive device having the following S-matrix, with necessary diagram.
$[S] = \begin{bmatrix} 0&0&1&1 \\ 0&0&-1&1 \\ 1&-1&0&0 \\ 1&1&0&0 \end{bmatrix}$
\end{asked}
\lead{Given}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A $4\times4$ matrix, so the device has \textbf{four ports}.
  \item \mk{The paper prints the first row as ``0 .0 1 1''}; the stray point is a scanner
    artefact over a zero, and the matrix is the standard form.
\end{itemize}
\par\penalty0\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\lead{Step 1: run the four tests}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.15cm}L{5.3cm}L{2.2cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Test} & \thead{Computation} & \thead{Result} & \thead{Conclusion} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Diagonal} & $S_{11}=S_{22}=S_{33}=S_{44}=0$ & all zero
  & \mk{Matched at every port} \\
\textbf{Transpose} & $S_{13}=S_{31}=1$, $S_{23}=S_{32}=-1$, $S_{14}=S_{41}=1$,

  & Phases are consistent; only the scale is wrong \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Judging the condition}: divide the printed matrix by $\sqrt{2}$. Then
    every column sums to $\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2} = 1$ and the device is
    \textbf{lossless}. \mk{Say this explicitly}: it is the ``judge the condition'' mark.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 2: map the zeros and read the signs}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Zero blocks}: $S_{12} = S_{21} = 0$, so \textbf{ports 1 and 2 are
    isolated}; $S_{34} = S_{43} = 0$, so \textbf{ports 3 and 4 are isolated}. Two
    isolated pairs on a matched, lossless, reciprocal 4-port.
  \item \textbf{Column 3} reads $(1, -1)$ down the collinear ports: the signs
    \textbf{flip}, so port 3 is the \textbf{difference (E) arm}.
  \item \textbf{Column 4} reads $(1, 1)$: the signs \textbf{match}, so port 4 is the
    \textbf{sum (H) arm}.
  \item \textbf{Equal magnitudes} everywhere off the zero blocks, so every split is
    $50/50$.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Answer}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\boxed{\text{The device is a magic tee (hybrid tee)}}$, with ports 1 and 2 the
    collinear arms, port 3 the E-arm and port 4 the H-arm. Normalised:
    \[ [S] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}
       0&0&1&1 \\ 0&0&-1&1 \\ 1&-1&0&0 \\ 1&1&0&0 \end{bmatrix} \]
  \item Diagram: reproduce the 3D sketch of Problem 5 with all four arms labelled.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Properties, one line each}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Matched} at all four ports, so no port reflects: $S_{ii}=0$, return loss
    infinite in the ideal case.
  \item \textbf{Reciprocal}, $[S]=[S]^{T}$: passive waveguide, no ferrite, no gain.
  \item \textbf{Lossless} once normalised: all incident power is redistributed, none
    dissipated.
  \item \textbf{Two isolated pairs}: $1$--$2$ and $3$--$4$. Two sources on ports 1 and 2
    cannot see each other, and the E-arm and H-arm are decoupled.
  \item \textbf{Equal power division}, $|S_{13}|^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}$: every drive splits in
    half, i.e. a $3$ dB hybrid.
  \item \textbf{Phase}: E-arm drive gives antiphase outputs, H-arm drive gives in-phase
    outputs.
  \item \textbf{Uses}: power combiner and splitter, duplexer, balanced mixer, monopulse
    comparator, impedance bridge.
\end{itemize}

\Q{Problem 9: Magic tee with both E- and H-arms short-circuited}{\m{4+4}}
{\color{sub}\footnotesize \yr{\textbf{\texttt{82 Bh}}} \gap$\cdot$\gap \added{} no source note carries this; derived here and asserted in \code{sparam.py}}

\begin{asked}{\textbf{\texttt{82 Bh}} \ [4+4]}
Design a model of a three-port network and define its characteristic parameters. Prepare the S-matrix of a rectangular magic tee having both E- and H-arms shorted, and explain how it behaves.
\end{asked}
\lead{Given}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The ideal magic tee of Problem 5, with ports 3 (E-arm) and 4 (H-arm)
    \textbf{short-circuited at the junction reference plane}.
  \item A short reflects with $\Gamma = -1$, so $a_3 = -b_3$ and $a_4 = -b_4$.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 1: write out the four scattering equations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item From $[b] = [S][a]$ with the magic-tee matrix:
    \[ b_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_3 + a_4), \qquad
       b_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(-a_3 + a_4) \]
    \[ b_3 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_1 - a_2), \qquad
       b_4 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_1 + a_2) \]
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 2: substitute the short-circuit conditions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $a_3 = -b_3 = -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_1 - a_2)$.
  \item $a_4 = -b_4 = -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_1 + a_2)$.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 3: eliminate and collect}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Into the expression for $b_1$:
    \[ b_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_1-a_2)
             - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_1+a_2)\right]
           = -\tfrac{1}{2}\left[(a_1-a_2) + (a_1+a_2)\right]
           = -\tfrac{1}{2}(2a_1) = -a_1 \]
  \item Into the expression for $b_2$:
    \[ b_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_1-a_2)
             - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_1+a_2)\right]
           = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[(a_1-a_2) - (a_1+a_2)\right]
           = \tfrac{1}{2}(-2a_2) = -a_2 \]
  \item The $a_2$ terms cancel in $b_1$ and the $a_1$ terms cancel in $b_2$: the two
    reflections arriving from the shorted arms add in the driven arm and subtract in the
    other one.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Answer}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Seen from the two collinear arms, the shorted magic tee is the two-port
    \[ \boxed{\;[S]_{\text{shorted}} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\;} \]
\end{itemize}
\lead{How it behaves}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Total reflection}: $|S_{11}| = |S_{22}| = 1$, so every watt fed into
    port 1 comes back out of port 1. Return loss $= 0$ dB, VSWR $= \infty$.
  \item \textbf{Phase reversal}: the $-1$ is a $180^\circ$ shift, exactly what an ideal
    short does. The tee behaves as a \textbf{pair of independent short circuits}, one at
    each collinear arm.
  \item \textbf{Complete isolation survives}: $S_{12} = S_{21} = 0$, so the two arms still
    cannot see each other even though both are totally reflecting.
  \item \textbf{Still lossless}: each column sums to $|-1|^2 = 1$; nothing is dissipated,
    everything is returned.
  \item \textbf{Still reciprocal}: $[S] = [S]^{T}$.
  \item \textbf{Use}: a reflective phase shifter or a calibration short. Moving the shorts
    a distance $\ell$ out from the junction changes $\Gamma$ from $-1$ to
    $-e^{-2j\beta\ell}$, and the same algebra then gives
    $S_{11} = S_{22} = -e^{-2j\beta\ell}$ with $S_{12}$ still zero, so a tunable phase
    with a constant unity magnitude.
\end{itemize}

\Q{Problem 10: Two radar transmitters, twice the power into one antenna}{\m{6}\m{8}}
{\color{sub}\footnotesize \yr{\textbf{73 Bh}, 74 Ma} \gap$\cdot$\gap a design question answered entirely by one matrix multiplication}

\begin{asked}{\textbf{73 Bh} \ [8] \quad and \quad 74 Ma \ [6]}
Suppose there are two identical radar transmitters and a few passive devices in equipment stock. A particular application requires twice more input power to an antenna than either transmitter can deliver. As an RF engineer, give your appropriate solution with necessary figures, mathematics and sufficient explanation.
\end{asked}
\lead{Given}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Two identical transmitters, each able to deliver $P$ watts.
  \item One antenna, which must receive $2P$ watts.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 1: choose the device, and say why}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Choice}: a \textbf{magic tee} used as a power \textbf{combiner}
    (a $3$ dB hybrid coupler works identically).
  \item \textbf{Why not a simple T-junction or Y-junction}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item A plain junction gives $S_{12} \neq 0$, so each transmitter drives power into
      the other one: mutual loading, pulling of the oscillators, and possible damage.
    \item Its collinear ports are not matched ($S_{11} = S_{22} = \tfrac{1}{2}$ for a tee,
      Problem 3), so both transmitters see a high VSWR.
  \end{itemize}
  \item The magic tee cures both: $S_{12} = 0$ (perfect isolation) and $S_{ii} = 0$
    (matched at every port).
\end{itemize}
\par\penalty0\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\lead{Step 2: the connection}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.4cm}L{3.4cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Port} & \thead{Connected to} & \thead{Reason} \\
\midrule
1 (collinear) & Transmitter A & Isolated from port 2 \\
2 (collinear) & Transmitter B & Isolated from port 1 \\
3 (E-arm)     & Matched dummy load & Takes the \textbf{difference}, which is zero when the
  drives are balanced \\
4 (H-arm)     & Antenna & Takes the \textbf{sum} of the two drives \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The two feed lines must be cut to the \textbf{same electrical length}, so that the
    transmitters arrive in phase.
\end{itemize}
\par\penalty0\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\lead{Step 3: the mathematics}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Both transmitters equal and in phase, nothing incident on the side arms:
    \[ [a] = \begin{bmatrix} a & a & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}^{T},
       \qquad |a|^2 = P \]
  \item Multiply $[b] = [S][a]$ with the magic-tee matrix:
    \[ b_3 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_1 - a_2) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a - a) = 0 \]
    \[ b_4 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_1 + a_2) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(2a) = \sqrt{2}\,a \]
    \[ b_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_3 + a_4) = 0, \qquad
       b_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(-a_3 + a_4) = 0 \]
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 4: read the powers}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.4cm}L{3.6cm}L{2.0cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Port} & \thead{Wave} & \thead{Power} & \thead{Meaning} \\
\midrule
4, antenna & $b_4 = \sqrt{2}\,a$ & $|b_4|^2 = 2|a|^2 = 2P$
  & \mk{The requirement, met exactly} \\
3, dummy load & $b_3 = 0$ & $0$ & Nothing wasted when the drives are balanced \\
1 and 2, transmitters & $b_1 = b_2 = 0$ & $0$ & No reflection back into either transmitter \\
\midrule
\textbf{Total out} & & $\mathbf{2P}$ & Equals the total in, as a lossless network requires \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\lead{Answer}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\boxed{\text{Feed the two transmitters into the collinear arms of a magic tee,
    antenna on the H-arm, matched load on the E-arm}}$. The antenna then receives
    $2P$ watts, twice what either transmitter alone can deliver.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Explanation, which carries the remaining marks}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Why the sum appears at the H-arm}: an H-plane junction excites the two
    collinear arms in phase, so by reciprocity two in-phase drives add there.
  \item \textbf{Why the dummy load matters}: if the transmitters are unequal or out of
    phase, $b_3 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_1 - a_2) \neq 0$ and the imbalance is
    \textbf{absorbed} by the load instead of being reflected into the transmitters. The
    load must be rated for the worst-case imbalance, i.e. the full power of one
    transmitter.
  \item \textbf{Phase error costs power}: with a phase error $\phi$ between the drives,
    the antenna receives $2P\cos^2(\phi/2)$ and the load takes the rest. A $20^\circ$
    error already loses $0.13$ dB.
  \item \textbf{Alternatives}: a $3$ dB branch-line or rat-race hybrid does the same job in
    microstrip; a Wilkinson combiner does it with an isolation resistor when the device
    must be planar.
\end{itemize}

\Q{Problem 11: ASR duplexer delivering half the transmitter power}{\m{8}\m{10}}
{\color{sub}\footnotesize \yr{73 Ma, \textbf{76 Bh}} \gap$\cdot$\gap the same magic tee, driven from the other end}

\begin{asked}{\textbf{76 Bh} \ [10] \quad and \quad 73 Ma \ [8]}
A radar installation engineer is given the responsibility of installing an Airport Surveillance Radar that requires half the input power to the antenna than the transmitter can deliver, using a duplexer. Prepare its S-matrix. \quad (73 Ma: choose a suitable passive microwave device to split power in half and explain its properties.)
\end{asked}
\lead{Given}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A transmitter delivering $P$ watts.
  \item The antenna must receive $P/2$ watts, and the receiver must share the same antenna
    while staying isolated from the transmitter.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 1: choose the device}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Choice}: a \textbf{magic tee used as a duplexer}. It is the only passive
    device that splits power exactly in half \emph{and} provides the transmitter-to-receiver
    isolation a duplexer must have.
  \item A resistive splitter would also halve the power but would burn the other half and
    give no isolation.
\end{itemize}
\par\penalty0\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\lead{Step 2: the connection}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.4cm}L{3.4cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Port} & \thead{Connected to} & \thead{Reason} \\
\midrule
4 (H-arm)     & Transmitter & Drives both collinear arms equally and in phase \\
1 (collinear) & Antenna & Receives half the transmitter power \\
2 (collinear) & Matched dummy load & Absorbs the other half, and balances the bridge \\
3 (E-arm)     & Receiver & Isolated from the H-arm, so the transmit pulse never reaches it \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\lead{Step 3: transmit path}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Drive vector $[a] = \begin{bmatrix} 0&0&0&a \end{bmatrix}^{T}$ with $|a|^2 = P$.
  \item From $[b]=[S][a]$:
    \[ b_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}a_4 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}a, \qquad
       b_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}a_4 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}a, \qquad
       b_3 = 0 \]
  \item Powers:
    \[ |b_1|^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}|a|^2 = \frac{P}{2}, \qquad
       |b_2|^2 = \frac{P}{2}, \qquad |b_3|^2 = 0 \]
  \item \mk{The antenna receives exactly $P/2$}, which is the requirement, and the
    receiver on the E-arm receives \textbf{nothing} from the transmitter, which is the
    point of a duplexer.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 4: receive path}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The echo arrives at port 1 alone: $[a] = \begin{bmatrix} a_1&0&0&0
    \end{bmatrix}^{T}$ with $|a_1|^2 = P_{echo}$.
  \item $b_3 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}a_1$ and $b_4 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}a_1$, so
    \[ |b_3|^2 = \frac{P_{echo}}{2} \quad\text{to the receiver}, \qquad
       |b_4|^2 = \frac{P_{echo}}{2} \quad\text{back into the transmitter} \]
  \item \textbf{Nothing reaches port 2}, since $S_{21} = 0$.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Answer}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The duplexer is the magic tee, whose S-matrix is
    \[ \boxed{\;[S] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}
       0 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 & 0
       \end{bmatrix}\;} \]
    with port 4 the transmitter, port 1 the antenna, port 2 the matched load and port 3
    the receiver.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Properties and the honest limitation}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Equal split}: $|S_{14}|^2 = |S_{24}|^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}$, a $3$ dB hybrid.
  \item \textbf{Isolation}: transmitter (port 4) to receiver (port 3) is $S_{34} = 0$, so
    the receiver front end is protected from the full transmit pulse.
  \item \textbf{Matched at every port}, so neither the transmitter nor the antenna sees a
    standing wave.
  \item \mk{The $3$ dB penalty is real and should be stated}: half the transmit power goes
    into the dummy load, and half the echo goes back to the transmitter. The magic-tee
    duplexer therefore costs $3$ dB in each direction.
  \item \textbf{Better in practice}: a \textbf{ferrite circulator} duplexer,
    $[S] = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, routes
    the \emph{whole} transmit power to the antenna and the \emph{whole} echo to the
    receiver, with no $3$ dB loss. Quote it as the improvement whenever the question does
    not explicitly demand the half-power split, as \textbf{76 Bh} does.
\end{itemize}

\Q{Problem 12: Evaluate the S-matrix of an amplitude modulator}{\m{7}}
{\color{sub}\footnotesize \yr{\textbf{78 Ch}} \gap$\cdot$\gap \added{} no source note carries this; modelled and evaluated here}

\begin{asked}{\textbf{78 Ch} \ [3+7]}
Elaborate the importance of S-parameters for microwave frequencies. Evaluate the S-matrix of an Amplitude Modulator.
\end{asked}
\lead{Step 1: model the modulator as a two-port}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A microwave amplitude modulator sits \textbf{in} the RF path: the carrier enters
    port 1 and the modulated carrier leaves port 2. The modulating signal is a
    \textbf{control input}, applied as bias, not as a third RF port.
  \item Physically it is a \textbf{PIN-diode variable attenuator}: the diode's RF
    resistance is set by its DC bias, so the modulating waveform steers the transmission
    through the device.
  \item Both RF ports are designed to stay \textbf{matched} across the modulation swing,
    otherwise the modulation would pull the source and produce incidental phase
    modulation.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 2: impose the three properties}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{3.0cm}L{2.8cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Property} & \thead{Equation} & \thead{Why} \\
\midrule
Matched at both ports & $S_{11} = S_{22} = 0$
  & The attenuator is built as a matched pad at every bias point \\
Reciprocal & $S_{12} = S_{21} = \alpha$
  & Passive, no ferrite and no gain: a diode resistance is bilateral \\
Lossy, and \textbf{time varying} & $0 \le \alpha(t) \le 1$
  & The power removed from the carrier is dissipated in the diode \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Hence
    \[ \boxed{\;[S] = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \alpha(t) \\ \alpha(t) & 0 \end{bmatrix},
       \qquad \alpha(t) = \alpha_0\left(1 + m\cos\omega_m t\right)\;} \]
    with $\alpha_0$ the carrier-level transmission, $m$ the modulation index and
    $\omega_m$ the modulating frequency.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 3: check the condition}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Column sum: $|S_{11}|^2 + |S_{21}|^2 = \alpha^2$, which equals 1 only when
    $\alpha = 1$.
  \item So the modulator is \mk{never lossless} except at full transmission: the
    dissipated fraction is $1 - \alpha^2(t)$, and this is unavoidable. An amplitude
    modulator works by \textbf{throwing power away} in step with the modulating signal.
  \item It is reciprocal and matched, so the only non-ideal property is the loss.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Step 4: evaluate with numbers}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Take $\alpha_0 = 0.5$ and $m = 0.6$, a typical shallow modulation.
\end{itemize}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.6cm}L{3.6cm}L{2.0cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\hrow \thead{Instant} & \thead{$\alpha(t)$} & \thead{$[S]$} & \thead{$IL$ and $P_{out}$ for $1$ W in} \\
\midrule
Peak, $\cos\omega_m t = +1$ & $0.5(1+0.6) = 0.80$
  & $\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&0.8\\0.8&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]$
  & $IL = -20\log 0.8 = 1.94$ dB; $P_{out} = 0.64$ W \\
Carrier, $\cos\omega_m t = 0$ & $0.5$
  & $\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&0.5\\0.5&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]$
  & $IL = 6.02$ dB; $P_{out} = 0.25$ W \\
Trough, $\cos\omega_m t = -1$ & $0.5(1-0.6) = 0.20$
  & $\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&0.2\\0.2&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]$
  & $IL = 13.98$ dB; $P_{out} = 0.04$ W \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Recovering the modulation index from the matrix}, which is the check the
    examiner is looking for:
    \[ m = \frac{|S_{21}|_{\max} - |S_{21}|_{\min}}{|S_{21}|_{\max} + |S_{21}|_{\min}}
         = \frac{0.80 - 0.20}{0.80 + 0.20} = \frac{0.60}{1.00} = 0.6 \checkmark \]
  \item \textbf{Modulation depth in dB}: the transmission swings over
    $13.98 - 1.94 = 12.04$ dB.
\end{itemize}
\lead{Notes worth adding}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Balanced realisation}: build the modulator on a magic tee, carrier into the
    H-arm, matched PIN diodes in the two collinear arms and the modulating bias on the
    E-arm. The two diodes reflect equally, the reflections recombine into the E-arm and
    the output amplitude follows the bias. The device is then a magic tee with two
    variable terminations, and the algebra is Problem 9 with $\Gamma$ set by the bias
    instead of fixed at $-1$.
  \item \textbf{$S_{11}$ is the figure of merit}: a good modulator keeps $S_{11}$ small at
    \emph{every} bias point. If it does not, the source sees a varying load and the output
    picks up incidental phase modulation.
  \item \textbf{Contrast with an ideal switch}: $\alpha$ toggling between $1$ and $0$ makes
    the same matrix an on-off (ASK) modulator, with $m = 1$.
\end{itemize}
